## 四、ClickHouse 实战教训：20 亿行/天的可观测性 torture test

**ClickHouse Will Let You Be Wrong at Scale** — Mat Duggan，2026-10-20

Duggan 分享了用 ClickHouse 处理 **20 亿行/天**网络防火墙遥测数据的完整踩坑记录，核心教训：

> "ClickHouse 是你遇到过的最随和的同事——它从不告诉你做错了，它只是让你错得离谱。"

**关键陷阱**：
1. **手写 WHERE 过滤器 vs Grafana ad-hoc 过滤器**：前者读取行数多 **84 倍**（257M vs 3M），因为守卫条件阻止了主键剪枝
2. **ORDER BY 设计错误无法修复**：`MODIFY ORDER BY` 只能追加列，不能重排——排错了意味着建新表、回填 520 GiB、原子切换
3. **Projection 路由是"带照片名单的保镖"**：`toStartOfHour(Timestamp)` 和 `toStartOfInterval(Timestamp, INTERVAL 1 HOUR)` 数学等价，但后者不会被路由——同样的查询，2022 行 vs 1237 万行
4. **基准测试陷阱**：不小心拿 NVMe 上的新表和已降级到 S3 的旧表对比，"法拉利 vs 拖拉机"

**唯一的朋友**：`system.query_log`——系统中唯一会告诉你"你搞砸了"的东西。

**为什么重要**：ClickHouse 正在赢得可观测性市场，但它的"沉默失败"特性意味着没有验证习惯就寸步难行。这对任何评估 OLAP 方案的团队都是必读。

## 六、cks：本地脚本的 manpage vs usage message——实用主义获胜

**My views on manual pages and usage messages for local scripts and tools** — Chris Siebenmann (cks)，2026-10-20

Unix 老兵 cks 坦诚自己对本地脚本 manpage 的矛盾心理：

**理想**：每个工具都有 manpage，文档完整规范
**现实**：
- manpage 和脚本是**两个独立工件**，必须同步维护——"理论上可行，实践中反复出问题"
- usage message **内嵌在程序里**，不可能忘记更新，且可以逐步完善
- 维护 manpage 需要特定专业知识 + 持续投入，"我工作过的任何地方都没真正做到过"

他的结论：**把简单文档嵌入程序就停在那里**——更有韧性、工作量更少、更容易可靠地实现。

**为什么重要**：这是一个关于"文档纪律"的诚实反思。在小团队/独立开发者场景中，选择"更容易维护"的方案往往比"理论上更优雅"的方案更能存活。

## 九、给创作者 / 独立开发者的启发

1. **AI 泡沫是结构性的，不是叙事性的** — Zitron 的数据说明：46.5% 的 NASDAQ 100 集中在 9 只 AI 相关股票。如果你的收入或投资组合与 AI 相关（大概率是），你需要思考"如果 AI 估值回调 30%，我的处境如何？"

2. **验证习惯是唯一的护城河** — ClickHouse 的教训适用于所有工具链：不要相信"它看起来在工作"，要建立系统性的验证流程。对 AI 生成内容同样适用。

3. **Google 搜索流量将进一步萎缩** — Doctorow 的判断正在变成现实。内容创作者需要加速建立**直接触达渠道**（Newsletter、RSS、社群），而不是继续依赖 Google 这个中间商。

4. **AI 编程的上限在迭代，不在生成** — Battle Drones 的成功不是因为"AI 能写 RTS"，而是因为 Simon 愿意 playtest 12 轮、给具体反馈、持续迭代。AI 放大的是**迭代速度**，不是**初始质量**。

5. **证书安全是一个被忽视的日常风险** — eMudhra 事件提醒我们：基础设施工具（Python、Node、curl）的证书链需要自动更新机制，否则某天醒来所有 HTTPS 请求都可能被静默拦截。

AI Had Better Work Out

Ed Zitron
Oct 20, 2026
31 min read

[Premium newsletter promotion section...]

Soundtrack: Royal Blood — Pull Me Through

At the end of July, Apollo Chief Economist Torsten Slok gave a blunt warning to the Washington Post:

"This AI thing better work out because if it doesn't ... we're going to have a problem," said Torsten Slok, chief economist at investment firm Apollo Global Management.

Today, I'm going to quantify exactly how difficult it will be for the "AI thing" to "work out."

As I've said before:

…everybody wants to make the AI bubble really complex, it's actually super simple: hundreds of billions of dollars are being invested to make single-digit billions of dollars maybe, some day, if AI data centers actually get built at scale and OpenAI and Anthropic can afford to pay for their compute.

The theoreticals and hypotheticals of this industry are so powerful that they've created one of the (if not the) largest equities bubbles in modern history, all heavily-centralized around the valuations of a handful of names related to the so-called AI trade, which translates to "buying or selling stuff to do with AI GPUs."

This has led to the single-highest inflow of foreign investments into US equities in history — larger than the Dot-Com Bubble, the leadup to the Great Financial Crisis, and even the dying gasps of the Zero Interest Free Era in 2021, per Apollo:

However you might hand-wave away the AI bubble's economic issues by saying it's "just like the Dot-Com Bubble" — when the NASDAQ 100 fell 77% from its peak — the consequences are now global, at a scale unseen in history, flowing into both direct stocks and indices.

NVIDIA, Google, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, AMD, and CoreWeave Represent 46.5% of the NASDAQ 100 (and 39.3% of the NASDAQ Composite), With Values Inflated By The "AI Trade"

As of typing this sentence, NVIDIA alone makes up 11.31% of the NASDAQ 100, with the combined weight of NVIDIA, Google, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, AMD and CoreWeave representing about 46.5% of the index's value, or 39.3% of the entire 3000-company NASDAQ composite.

These same names — replacing CoreWeave with Oracle — represent around 28.9% of the value of the S&P 500.

To sustain the valuation of these stocks — let alone continue growing — will require every company involved to either spend or make hundreds of billions of dollars in perpetuity, increasing every single quarter, without fail. This will come from either selling ever-increasing amounts of AI chips and related hardware, or buying and using them to generate revenue either through rentals or building their own software products.

While the centralization was more severe at the top of the Dot-Com era NASDAQ — with Cisco, Intel and Microsoft accounting for roughly 74% — the total value of the index was a mere $1.82 trillion ($3.58 trillion adjusted for inflation).

Today's top three — NVIDIA, Google and Microsoft — account for around 29% of the NASDAQ 100, for a total market capitalization of $13.5 trillion.

I'm afraid things only get worse.

Retail Investors Own 10% Of The Market, With 38%+ US Household Wealth Held In Stocks, and Hold at least 22.4% of NVIDIA and 21.7% of Microsoft's Traded Stock

Per Goldman Sachs (via Reuters), "...equity gains have been the dominant driver of household wealth accumulation and the main contributor to a positive wealth effect on consumer spending," and per data from the Fed, corporate equities accounted for 37.9% of all household wealth in Q2 2026, the highest number on record since Q4 2000.

Economist Paul Kedrosky told me it might actually be higher:

Americans are near records in terms of equity exposure, over 50% [as in number of Americans holding stocks], ahead of real estate for the first time since WWII — and Americans are at record levels in terms of equities as a percentage of their liquid net worth — over 60%.

While the Fed's data only goes back to Q4 2000, corporate equities as a share of household wealth only sat at 25.9% ($11.5 trillion, or $22.7 trillion adjusted for inflation), back at a time when total household wealth sat at $44.4 trillion, or about $85.2 trillion in today's money. In Q2 2026, total household wealth sits at about $195.8 trillion, with corporate equities sitting at roughly $74 trillion, or roughly 86.8% of the entire US household wealth 26 years ago.

Retails are also making up an historically-large amount of the volume of trades on the market, per Bloomberg Intelligence, making up 20.3% of trading in 2026 to date, approaching the dangerous heights of the worst excesses of the end of the Zero Interest Rate Policy era.

Retail Investors Have Over $4.65 Trillion In Exposure To AI-Related Stocks

Retail investors also directly own about 29% of Amazon's, 26.2% of NVIDIA's, 22.6% of AMD's, 21.8% of Microsoft's, and 18.6% of Meta's stocks, though these numbers are not inclusive of index funds like SPY or QQQ (a fund tied to the NASDAQ). In totality, retail investors own equity assets (at least as of May) equivalent to about 10% of the market.

In a note to clients, analyst Daniel Chavez explained that Goldman Sachs trading desk data shows retail volumes have risen 28% since mid-April, with a basket of retail-favorite stocks tracked by the firm gaining 29% over the same period.

By comparison, Dot-Com era retail trading was much, much lower — less than 2% of the NYSE's trading volume according to the University of Michigan's Alicia J. Davis in a paper that begins with the phrase "the American retail investor is dying."

When all of this is over, I think retail investors might wish they were dead, as they directly own somewhere in the region of $4.65 trillion worth of NVIDIA, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Broadcom, AMD, Micron, Oracle, and CoreWeave stocks. The number is likely much smaller than the true exposure retail investors have, as it isn't inclusive of investments in index funds.

Sidenote: For some comparison, the total market capitalization of Microsoft, Cisco and Intel on March 10, 2000 (the peak of the market) was around $1.36 trillion.

Another point is that, unlike 2000, retail investors have easy access to exotic securities contracts that weren't previously available (or, if they were available, were expensive or otherwise obscured from the casual view of most retail investors), and that have the potential to amplify any potential losses.

RobinHood, for example, allows retail investors to buy event contracts (where "investors" speculate on an event with a yes/no outcome), to trade futures, to short stock, and to invest on margin, or with borrowed money. All of this can be done (assuming the investor meets the criteria to access these products) with a few taps on a phone, effectively eliminating the friction associated with making a potentially life-altering financial decision.

The recognition that many technology stocks can't possibly grow quickly enough to merit the heights they achieved through momentum investing may have pierced the bubble of overvaluation in recent weeks, analysts said, as momentum changed direction. While it seems unlikely, a drop of around 20% across NVIDIA, Amazon, Google, Meta, Broadcom, AMD, Oracle, Micron, and CoreWeave would have the same effect as the entire market downturn on the worst day of the Dot-Com Bubble crash.

### C. Cory Doctorow：The death of history's most successful publisher（完整原文）

```text
The death of history's most successful publisher

I published my first story in 1988, when I was 17 years old. Today, at 55, I've published more than 30 books, with three more coming in the next year or so. In the intervening years, I've spent a lot of time thinking and talking about publishing. I've had plenty of opportunity to reflect on publishing: I've been a bookseller and worked in libraries, I wrote a column for Publishers Weekly, self-published a few books of my own, published the first-ever book licensed under Creative Commons, and broke several Kickstarter records for audiobook projects.

But most of all, I've worked with publishing professionals: publicists, marketing directors, operations people, copyeditors, and, above all, editors. My first novel editor was the magnificent Patrick Nielsen Hayden, who supported my publishing career from the very start, donating to help send me to the Clarion science fiction writers' workshop and inviting me to come read slush at Tor Books whenever I was in New York.

Patrick didn't teach me everything I know about publishing, but he sure taught me a lot about publishing, and hardly a day goes by without my having cause to reflect on one of our many discussions over our decades-long professional and personal association.

Out of all the things I learned about publishing from Patrick, one truth stands out above all: publishing is "the act of making something public." Publishing may or may not involve editing, layout, printing, binding, marketing or other activities that we associate with publishing houses, but publishing always involves identifying a work, identifying an audience, and finding ways to connect that work to that audience.

This definition does a lot of useful work. For one thing, it locates publishing as part of the hard problem of human society, namely, "getting people to care about something that matters to you." Beyond publishing, this is the crux of politics, religion and commerce.

For another, this definition lets us understand that (for most of its existence) Google was the most successful publisher in human history. No other institution of any kind connected more works with more audiences. Lots of people have heard of Google's wildly unsuccessful motto "Don't be evil," but far fewer are familiar with Google's wildly successful mission statement, "organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful."

But all of that is in the past tense. With its AI projects – AI summaries, the Gemini chatbot – Google has abandoned the practice of "connecting works with audiences." Today, Google's function is to plunder all the works on the internet and chop them up and arrange them in an unimaginably vast, multidimensional array for the explicit purpose of preventing audiences from connecting with those works.

For most of its history, Google operated in a virtuous cycle with publishers whereby they allowed Google to crawl their sites and Google located audiences and connected them with the pages on those sites. Today, that cycle has turned vicious, with Google acting as a parasite that extracts value from the web's publishers and offers nothing in return.

This is entirely in keeping with the enshittification hypothesis: that once a platform is liberated from the disciplining forces of competition, regulation, labor and interoperability, it will squeeze its suppliers and users and harvest all the value for itself.

But AI represents a new phase in enshittification. Google has assayed enshittificatory gambits in the past, like deliberately worsening its search results in order to force you to search repeatedly, which gave the company more chances to show you ads. But as sleazy as those gambits were, they were profitable. In some important sense, they were sustainable. Assuming Google could carry on indefinitely scamming its users in this way, they'd continue to bring in more cash than they spent, and continue to be the world's most successful publisher, connecting works and audiences (albeit in a deliberately degraded way).

Contrast this with AI, the money-losingest endeavor in the history of human civilization. Google is losing hundreds of billions of dollars on AI. The more AI Google does, the worse the company's financial footing becomes. If Google does this for long enough, it may cease to exist altogether.

This collapse can only be prolonged by Google's ongoing extraction of value from web publishers, who no longer have their works connected with audiences via Google search results. Instead, Google usurps those audiences, shows them as many ads as it can, and still loses money in the bargain.

In Tim Wu's Age of Extraction, Wu lays out a thesis about how intermediaries – middlemen who perform a useful function connecting different groups, firms and individuals – are prone to developing "main character syndrome," abandoning their role as facilitators for others and abusing their position to make themselves the prime beneficiary of the transactions they facilitate.

Google has a scorching case of main character syndrome, and nowhere is that more obvious than in its AI-fueled transformation into web-scale parasite. The fact that this gambit might well destroy Google and the publishers the company once served – rather than merely enshittifying search with worse results and more ads – makes the whole thing look more like a Mafia bust-out than the enshittification of the first half of the 2020s.

It's certainly an ignominious end for history's most successful publisher (as was).

### D. Mat Duggan：ClickHouse Will Let You Be Wrong at Scale（完整原文）

```text
ClickHouse Will Let You Be Wrong at Scale

October 20, 2026
10 min read
in Observability

So I've posted before how I think ClickHouse is great. Its columnar storage is fast, it has amazing compression. I won't bore you with those details, you can see them all here: https://matduggan.com/clickhouse-is-winning-the-observability-wars/

However all technology is great until you try to do something atypical with it.

What I wanted was a realistic torture test: network firewall telemetry, on the order of 2 billion rows a day of mixed-vendor syslog, parsed at ingest into wide typed tables with a 14-day TTL. The dashboards needed to do two things at once in Grafana: fast lookup of individual log lines, and fast visualizations where you can stack ad-hoc filters on top of each other across every panel simultaneously.

I learned a lot of things about ClickHouse. Also some things about myself, not all flattering.

TL;DR

This ended up being significantly harder than I thought it would be, and part of that is that I made a ton of mistakes. Don't email me mean comments. I'm writing this up as a warning to the people coming behind me, and I'll own every mistake as we go.

For the happy OTel path, CH is very easy. Few hundred metrics, couple million logs a day, several thousand traces, not a problem. For the more complex custom route, CH is extremely powerful but it's hard to use.

The theme of the whole project is that this stack fails silently. ClickHouse is the most agreeable coworker you'll ever have because it never says you are wrong, it just lets you be super wrong. A query that looks fine reads 84x more rows than it should. A misconfigured filter just doesn't apply. A projection that never routes still returns correct numbers, slowly. Once I understood that ClickHouse will never tell me that I did something wrong and that I had to figure out I did something wrong, we got along fine.

You have to build verification habits before you build anything else. system.query_log is your friend, your bartender, the lovable dog in a horror movie. This is the only thing in the system that says you messed up.

Here are the lessons I learned. Hopefully they save someone else from going down the same path I did.

Setup

So we're not doing anything weird here.

ClickHouse is a 2 shard x 3 replicas setup underneath the Altinity operator. Every table is a ReplicatedMergeTree wrapped in Distributed tables and Grafana only ever queries the wrapper.

Grafana is the official grafana-clickhouse-datasource plugin, 25 panels and then ad-hoc filters up at the top that apply to every panel at once.

Hard guardrails we cannot break. max_rows_to_read = 1 billion, max_bytes_to_read = 100 GB, max_execution_time = 60s, all in throw mode. I can raise these but the entire point of this process was to operate inside of the CH model for data and try to leave those alone.

Lesson 1: Don't handwrite the filters

My first draft of the dashboard wrote filters the way I'd typically write them in normal SQL, by adding a guard so the query stays valid regardless of what the user picks.

WHERE (empty({{observer}}) OR observer IN {{observer}})

This would end up being quite the stupid decision.

predicate                          rows read    time
observer = 'one-device' (direct)   3.05M        115 ms
(empty(...) OR col IN ...) guard   257.64M      1,134 ms

Eighty-four times the rows, ten times the time, same answer. Why is this so much worse? I'm not entirely sure. My best guess is that the guard keeps primary-key pruning from engaging, so you get a full partition scan with post-filtering. I clearly measured the effect but I'm not exactly sure how to prove the mechanism. EXPLAIN was my only savior here.

The fix was to stop putting in the WHERE-clause filtering by hand and use Grafana ad-hoc filtering. The key learning here is how the plugin does that and why it makes a difference. It does not rewrite your WHERE clause. It appends the filter as a ClickHouse query setting, additional_table_filters, which means it is applied at planning time and prunes exactly like a hand-written query.

Lesson 2: Sort keys matter sorta (dad joke, you are welcome)

When I first set up the main table, I did the following:

ORDER BY (observer, action, application, toStartOfHour(Timestamp), Timestamp)

The logic made sense at the time, even if it didn't end up holding up. We're sorting by network device first since of all of these, there are going to be the least number of network devices. But what I didn't really think about is that every panel is time-bound because of course they are, that's how Grafana works. In ClickHouse only the front of the sort key gets the fast lookup path. Obvious in retrospect, but stumped me for a hot minute. I thought I was structuring this by lowest cardinality (device) and then out to most cardinality, which logically made sense.

If all you want is information on the right way to do this, you can find that here: https://clickhouse.com/blog/working-with-time-series-data-and-functions-ClickHouse and https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-03-31-clickhouse-choose-order-by-key/view

What does ORDER BY mean in ClickHouse?

What's important to understand about this index is that it's not the same as like MySQL necessarily. It's the physical layout, the rows are written to disk in sorted order like a phone book sorted by lastname, firstname.

ClickHouse by design doesn't keep a very large "bookmark list", every 8192 rows (called a granule), it writes down the sort-key values at that spot. That bookmark list is the primary key.

So the Fast lookup only works for a prefix of the sort columns:
"All the Smiths" → jump straight to the S section. Fast.
"Everyone named John" → Johns are scattered through every last-name section → you read the whole book. Slow.

Their table also splits data into daily partitions (separate files per day). That's a coarse filter that works no matter what the sort key is (remember this, it becomes important)

Why is timestamp in there twice?

The two entries are not the same thing.

toStartOfHour(Timestamp) — the hour bucket. It rounds 14:37:22 down to 14:00:00. This is the "cutoff"
Timestamp — the exact time.

Within any fixed (observer, action, application) group, sorting by (hour, exact-time) produces exactly the same order as sorting by (exact-time) alone — the hour is just a rounded copy of the same value, so it can never re-order two rows.

So my first design was basically this (observer, action, application, hour, Timestamp).
So time sat at position four, and the most common predicate in the dashboard was aimed at the worst-positioned column. The tail-logs panel with ORDER BY Timestamp DESC LIMIT 1000, a multi-hour window, measured before the guardrails went up, read 291.8M rows, 158 GiB, in 18.9 seconds; with one observer filter, 23.1M rows, 936 ms. The newest rows sit at the end of each device's block so a newest-N query has to walk every device's block to find the tail.

The fix:

(toStartOfHour(Timestamp), observer, action, application, Timestamp) — puts the bucket first, so an hour range is one contiguous block. All the Smiths together.

Here's what makes this decision consequential: MODIFY ORDER BY can't reorder, prepend, or absorb existing columns. You can only append columns added in the same ALTER. Get the key wrong on day one and the fix is not an ALTER; it's a new table, ~520 GiB of backfill per shard under live ingest, and an atomic swap. Ask me how I know. MODIFY ORDER BY turns out to be less 'modify' and more 'build a second house next door, carry everything over by hand, then swap the mailboxes.

After all those lessons, I ended up running a benchmark that was the kind of beautiful baby benchmark you dream of where it confirms you solved every problem. An identical aggregate query, 156 ms vs 1,690 ms. 10.8x faster! I felt great about that for most of an afternoon, which is how long it took to notice that 88% of the old table's partition had tiered to S3 while the new table sat entirely on local NVMe. We had benchmarked a Ferrari against a tractor and concluded the tractor was slow. Which is true. It is also not the thing we measured. Excellent work.

The same-tier control with both on NVMe produced no noticeable query differences. So was it worth reorganizing? I'm not sure! I think?
This took a long time to do, so triple check this part.

Lesson 3: Projections are trickier than I thought

So when you first run across projections in ClickHouse, they seem pretty great.

A projection is not a separate table you manage. It's an alternative physical ordering of the same data, stored inside the same table. Think of it this way: your table is a book sorted by chapter number. A projection is an index at the back, sorted by topic.

When a query matches your projection, it should just automagically read from the projection instead of the base table. You don't need to change the query, which is great. New data populates the projection on INSERT, which is what you want for this sort of stuff.

Think of it like this:
Every table stores data in parts, which are directories on disk.
When you add a projection, each part directory gets a subdirectory for the projection data with an anonymous table with its own columns, index, granultes.
Projection parts merge at the same time as main table parts, so there's no lag like what you can experience with materialized views.
Projections don't backfill by default but you can backfill them, which is a mistake that I made initially where I added one, nothing was better and I assumed I had messed up.

Projections are great for network firewall data because the data doesn't need to change shape. I just need to optimize my querying of the data for specific usecases.

The biggest misunderstanding about how projections worked is that I made a lot of assumptions about how expression matching worked. I assumed (for no good reason) that expression matching was loose and that CH would do a lot of work to ensure my query was routed to the projection. count() and sum(duration) won't be used by a query asking for avg(duration), which didn't occur to me until I read this: https://obsessiondb.com/blog/clickhouse-projections-complete-guide

Also every filter column must be in the projection's GROUP BY. This means a filter on an absent column → full fallback to raw. This was a fucking nightmare to debug until I figured this out.

WHERE clause                              routed?   rows read
(none)                                    yes       2,022
toStartOfHour(Timestamp) >= X             yes       2,022
toStartOfInterval(Timestamp, INTERVAL 1 HOUR) >= X  no  12,373,248
Timestamp >= X (raw)                      no        12,373,248

Here are the basic lessons you must understand to do a large scale project.

The query's aggregates must be a subset of the projection's — count() and sum(duration) won't serve avg(duration).
Every filter column must be in the projection's GROUP BY — otherwise, silent fallback to raw.
The time predicate must use the projection's own expression: toStartOfHour(Timestamp) >= X routes; toStartOfInterval(Timestamp, INTERVAL 1 HOUR) >= X — identical values — does not. Same query: 2,022 rows versus 12,373,248. It doesn't reason about equivalence. It's a bouncer with a laminated list, and toStartOfInterval is toStartOfHour's identical twin in a different jacket.

All of this was figured out when I started just obsessively checking query_log. In order to use projections in ClickHouse you have to verify the routing every single time.

My views on manual pages and usage messages for local scripts and tools

October 19, 2026

I recently wrote about how you (we) should have verbose usage messages for (some) local scripts, and a commentator suggested having manpages for such scripts. It turns out that I have tangled feelings about such manpages and how they interact with (verbose) usage messages.

First off, I think that infrequently used local scripts and programs should probably keep verbose usage messages even if you have good local manual pages for them, because verbose usage messages seem likely to save a step. Without verbose usage messages, you may well wind up running the program once with incorrect usage, then reaching for 'man <program>', then re-running the program. With a verbose usage message you can hopefully cut one step out of this process, and it's probably not harmful even if people often read the manual page even after the verbose usage. The likely exception to this is if people pretty much always re-read a program's manual page if it's been a while since they use it. However, this is an ideal situation and one that requires ongoing work and commitment to achieve. Manual pages have to be written to go with the local tools (which means people need the expertise in the field), then they have to be installed along with the tools, possibly the system configuration will need to be tweaked to find them easily, and then both the master version and the installed copies (if the two are different) will need to be kept up to date with the program itself. Fundamentally a program and its manual page are two separate artifacts (files) that need to always be handled together; in theory this works, in practice, over and over again problems have ensued with this sort of coupled artifacts (especially in light of one of my aphorisms of system administration).

So my overall view is that embedding simple documentation in your local programs and stopping there is both more resilient and less work, making it more likely to happen reliably. It also degrades much more gradually than manual pages do; you can write a bit of a usage message or a help message in a way that you can't write and install a little bit of a manual page. If you have a local culture where you maintain expertise in writing and managing manual pages and you reliably spend the time to do so, this is great, but I think it's likely to be hard to develop and sustain such a culture. And you might want to spend that documentation time on things and forms other than manual pages for local tools, if your local tools can get by with quick, basic usage stuff.

One practical issue in this situation is that once you have both a script and a manual page for it, you probably want a source location where both are together in one directory and are version controlled together. When you have a script alone, you don't need this; you can put the script in some global location and then version control it there (with RCS, for example (but also)).

As a long term Unix person, the ideal of manual pages for everything appeals to me. As a practical system administrator, I have to acknowledge that none of the places I've worked at around the university have ever really done that, and I'm fairly sure that if we tried to start, it wouldn't work out. You need a certain sort of commitment to the ideal to persist even in the face of the frictions.

(Manual pages are not the platonic ideal form of program documentation that everything should always adhere to, either, but that's a discussion that doesn't fit in the margins of this entry.)
