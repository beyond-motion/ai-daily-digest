## 三、Cory Doctorow：广告技术（ad-tech）的政治学——监控 vs 情境

**The technopolitics of ad-tech** — Cory Doctorow / Pluralistic，2026-10-19

Doctorow 借用 Langdon Winner 的经典框架"Do Artifacts Have Politics?"，分析广告技术的三种政治性：

1. **故意嵌入政治**：如 Robert Moses 设计的低桥，故意阻止公交车（穷人/黑人）进入海滩
2. **意外但不可避免的政治**：如番茄采摘机，虽非故意，但必然导致工人失业、番茄品种单一化
3. **设计内在政治**：如太阳能板（分散式）vs 核电（集中式）的本质区别

他的核心论点：**广告技术（ad-tech）属于第二类——它的监控本质必然导致权力集中**。即使广告商本意只是"精准投放"，技术架构决定了它必须收集海量数据、建立行为画像，这不可避免地盘活了监控资本主义。

**为什么重要**：当 AI 与广告技术结合（如 Meta 的 AI 广告系统），这种权力集中将加速。Doctorow 提醒：技术设计不是中立的，它预设了特定的社会结构。

### B. Sean Goedecke：Laypeople support technical fields for technology, not art（完整原文）

```text
Laypeople support technical fields for technology, not art
October 20, 2026

Some people think that LLMs should not be used in technical fields like programming or mathematics, because those fields are actually art. True or not, this kind of argument is not going to be persuasive to the general population. The argument goes something like this:

It doesn't matter how effective LLMs are at technical fields like programming or mathematics, because one of the essential values of those fields is that they're art. While it looks to laypeople like these fields exist to solve practical problems, what they're actually for is to produce works of beauty, or to express something distinctively human, or to spend time contemplating the infinite. Simply solving the problems misses the point in the same way that building an infinite poem generator would miss the point of poetry.

I agree that programming or mathematics can be beautiful. I remember being mildly awestruck at learning epsilon-delta proofs, and I have written a lot about the kind of software I think is elegant. Since there's no clear definition of art or of aesthetics in general, it's plausible to me that almost everything could have an artistic dimension to it.

All of this is compelling to software engineers, many of whom (like me) find some programs beautiful and are flattered to think of themselves as artists. But I suspect it's spectacularly uncompelling to onlookers, because it undermines the implicit reasons that they have for supporting technical fields. Many people who are not programmers or mathematicians think of those fields like this:

- I enjoy the fruits of technology: modern medicine, transport, mobile phones, and so on
- While I don't understand the exact relationship, I know that technical fields like mathematics and programming are essential to sustaining and advancing these technologies
- Therefore I'm happy to respect and support mathematicians in their mysterious research work
- And I'm broadly okay with software engineers drawing healthy salaries for the same reason

In other words, regular people think mathematicians (and to a lesser extent programmers) are valuable because they think science is valuable, and they think science is valuable because they think technology is valuable. They are willing to accept that technical fields can be art only insofar as that eventually helps researchers have the kind of creative insights that advance science: as Rikab sarcastically puts it, that advance the knowledge frontier.

If you go to those non-technical people and you say "LLMs can accelerate problem-solving in these fields, but I'm strongly against using them because the real point is to produce great art", they will be very unhappy. They might reasonably feel cheated, in the same way as if they hired someone to renovate their kitchen who insisted in doing it all with hand tools in order to experience "the joy of creation". This argument — we should stop using LLMs in technical fields because they're art — is going to be even less compelling to technical employers.

Engineers and mathematicians who want to defend the artistic value of their field will need more compelling arguments than just appealing to the value of art. We will need to find some way to connect the value of art with the practical technological value that motivates public interest in our fields. For instance, we could argue that:

- Great software products must rely on elegant abstractions, which need a human creative touch: simply piling on working code will hit a wall past a certain level of complexity
- LLMs can solve problems, but actual progress in mathematics depends on creating new abstractions, which is a more creative task that LLMs are not yet good at
- Progress in technical fields is uneven and relies upon inspiration, which needs creative hammock time and distinctively human understanding
- LLMs can produce working software, but truly great software requires human inspiration, and great software is what advances the field

I don't know if I agree with all of these points. I haven't yet seen an LLM-created codebase truly hit a wall (at least, not this year), and I don't see a reason why LLMs should be incapable of creating new abstractions or having human-like inspirations. I also don't think great products have to have great code: why wouldn't workmanlike code in the hands of a great product team produce a great product?
```

### C. Cory Doctorow：The technopolitics of ad-tech（节选）

```text
The technopolitics of ad-tech (permalink)

"Technopolitics" are the politics embedded in technology. The Ur-text of technopolitics is "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" by the rock critic turned scholar Langdon Winner.

With "Do Artifacts," Winner demands that we move beyond the idea of technologies having "intended consequences" and "unintended consequences" and towards a political economy of tech. In service to this, he divides the politics of artifacts into three categories:

I. Artifacts that have politics embedded in them by their creators, like Robert Moses's infamous bridges over the Long Island parkways. According to Moses biographer Robert Caro, Moses deliberately specified that these bridges must be too low for a city bus to pass under, in a bid to block Black people from traveling from New York City to the beaches he'd developed on Long Island.

Today those bridges are more than a century old, and they are still too low for city buses to pass under, and this continues to mean that people who don't own private vehicles find it more difficult to get to the beach, and as it's still the case that race is a predictor of low incomes, those are still racist bridges.

II. Artifacts whose politics are accidental but unavoidable. Winner cites a kind of tomato harvester that vastly improved the efficiency of tomato pickers. These harvesters became very popular, which allowed tomato farmers to lay off large numbers of agricultural workers, and also allowed them to price the mechanically harvested tomatoes so cheaply that they crowded out other kinds of tomatoes.

Because these machines created a large pool of unemployed workers, they shifted power from labor to capital, and because these picking machines were too rough for all but the hardest varietals of tomato, they changed the flavor profile of tomatoes in America for the worse, towards a bland, tough tomato.

These picking machines were not designed to make workers poorer, nor were they designed to make tomatoes taste worse, but because of their technical characteristics, this was the unavoidable outcome, the inextricable politics of these machines.

III. Artifacts whose politics are intrinsic to their design. Here, Winner contrasts solar panels with nuclear power plants. Solar panels are decentralized and can be owned by individuals or small communities; nuclear plants are massive, centralized, and require state-level security and oversight. The politics of these technologies are not accidental; they are what the technologies are.

Ad-tech, Doctorow argues, belongs to category II: its surveillance architecture inevitably concentrates power, even if advertisers merely want to "target better." The collection of behavioral data, the creation of user profiles, the real-time bidding systems — these are not features that can be removed without destroying the technology itself. The politics is baked in.
```
