## 二、机器人训练数据从哪来？（Construction Physics / Brian Potter）

**How to Train Your Robot** — Brian Potter，2026-10-15（长文，~9 分钟）

LLM 的 Scaling Hypothesis 成功源于"现成的高质量文本足够多"（Epoch AI 估计 ≈ 300T token）。机器人领域没有这种现成数据 —— **核心问题：数据从哪来、要多少、要花多少钱**。

### 五种主流数据来源
1. **遥操作（teleoperation）**：人直接控制机器人完成任务。优点：数据完全匹配目标机器人本体；缺点：**贵**（要真机器人+人坐那儿）+ 没有触觉反馈 → 灵巧操作数据质量差。常见场景：数据工厂、"忘了在控制机器人"的意外事故。
2. **真机强化学习（RL）**：让机器人自己跑 + 打分。Google 2016 的 "arm farm"。被寄望于未来"数据飞轮"，但目前**真能做有用工作的机器人太少，数据高度重复**，目前贡献有限。Physical Intelligence 在巧克力工厂做原型。
3. **仿真 + 强化学习**：Figure AI 用仿真让人形机器人学会走路；OpenAI 用 "13,000 年" 仿真经验教机械手解魔方；Waymo 真实 10M 英里 vs 仿真 10B 英里。**最大问题：sim-to-real gap**（摩擦/弹性/软物体建模难）。代表技巧：**Domain Randomization / ADR**（OpenAI 用于魔方手）。魔方最终在最困难初始状态下成功率仅 20% —— 所以这条路目前不是主流。
4. **第一人称人类视频（egocentric video）**：给人戴头显录视频，把人的动作映射成机器人动作。这是**人形机器人形态流行**的原因之一。Figure Index 计划：每秒收集 30 分钟视频（= 每天 43,000 小时）；Shift 也类似。可加触觉手套（Neocambrian "Mitts"）→ 成本更低但**动作映射不完美**。
5. **UMI（Universal Manipulation Interface）**：人拿一对特制夹爪+摄像头做任务，录制视频直接当机器人训练数据。Generalist AI / Sunday Robotics 主要走这条路。是近期**最惊艳 demo 的来源之一**。

### 数据集规模对比（关键数字）
- Open X-Embodiment (Google DeepMind, 2023)：100 万条轨迹 / 4,000 小时 → 当时已算大。
- Physical Intelligence π0（2024-10）：**10,000 小时**。
- Generalist AI GEN 1：**500,000 小时 UMI 数据**。
- Figure Index：**每天 43,000 小时**（单公司）。
- Dyna Robotics / Nature Will：**>100 万小时**人类第一人称视频。
- 全行业估计：**每月 ~500,000 小时**。

**和 LLM 的差距**：以"一个人读完需要多久"做粗暴对比 —— GPT-2 ≈ 47.5 万小时、GPT-3 ≈ 2,600 万小时、GPT-4 ≈ **6.85 亿小时**。当前机器人数据 ≈ GPT-4 的 **1/1000**。Ken Goldberg 称之为"**100,000 年数据鸿沟**"。Chris Paxton 估算：每小时机器人视频 ≈ 3,600 有效 token → 100 万小时 ≈ 36 亿 token，比新模型训练集小 **5,000 倍**。

### 成本账
机器人数据市价 **$60–$150/小时**，内部工程优化后能压到 **$10/小时**（Pantheon）；Shift 给录数据的人开价 **$20/小时**。
**要补到 LLM 同等量级（~10 亿小时），粗算要烧 $10B–$100B**。

### 不确定性
- 也许我们不需要那么多（只要做特定任务不需要泛化）。
- 也许**通用大模型（Fable / Astra 类）本身就很强**，少量机器人数据 fine-tune 就够 —— 专用机器人模型可能会被通用模型替代。
- 也许**物理操作本质上比文本更难**：文本错一个字不影响整段，但机器人**位置错 1mm 就是成功 vs 完全失败**。

## 四、Token Ring：输给 Ethernet 的另一种可能（Silicon Underground / Dave Farquhar）

**What was a Token Ring network?** — Dave Farquhar，2026-10-15

- IBM 1985-10-15 首次发布 Token Ring 标准 —— **整整 41 年前的今天**。
- **与 Ethernet 的关键差异**：Token Ring 是 IBM 主导的封闭标准，Ethernet 是 DEC / 3Com 等推动的开放标准。
- **技术优势**：Token Ring 用"令牌环"机制避免冲突，比早期 Ethernet 的"碰撞+重传"更稳；处理网络饱和的能力强得多。
- **但有两个致命伤**：
  1. **贵** —— 一套设备价格是 Ethernet 的 3 倍。
  2. **所有网卡必须跑同一速度** —— 一台 4Mbps 设备接入 16Mbps 环**会拖垮整个网络**（作者亲历，"只犯过一次"）。
- **Ethernet 怎么赢的**： hubs → switches 解决了碰撞问题；**靠规模暴力升级到更高速率** —— 16M Token Ring > 10M Ethernet，但 100M Ethernet 出来后 Token Ring 就跟不上了；Gigabit Ethernet 在 128M Token Ring 上市前就落地。
- **今天的鉴别**：retro 计算爱好者拿到一块神秘 ISA 网卡，**后面板是 DE-9 接口的就是 Token Ring**（Ethernet 从不用 DE-9）。
- **教训**：**技术更好不等于赢**。开放标准 + 规模经济 + 生态迭代速度 > 单点技术优势。

### B. How to Train Your Robot（Brian Potter / Construction Physics）

```text
The enormous advance in AI capabilities over the last several years has in large part been driven by the availability of vast amounts of training data. Gwern’s influential 2020 essay “ The Scaling Hypothesis ,” written after the release of GPT-3, argues that recent advances in AI have been the result of scale: bigger models, bigger datasets, and more compute spent training them. Gwern writes: 
 The scaling hypothesis regards the blessings of scale as the secret of AGI: intelligence is ‘just’ simple neural units & learning algorithms applied to diverse experiences at a (currently) unreachable scale. As increasing computational resources permit running such algorithms at the necessary scale, the neural networks will get ever more intelligent. 

 Likewise, pseudonymous AI researcher Nostalgebraist wrote in 2024 that “the entire current landscape dates back to the realization that ML models improve steadily and predictably as you train them on more data. And that everything since then consists of training bigger models on larger datasets, enabled by years of rapid improvement in hardware.” Epoch AI found that since the early 2010s, the size of training datasets for AI language models has doubled about every six months . The biggest AI models today are trained on tens of trillions of tokens. 

 As more and more effort gets devoted to applying modern AI to robotics, an important question is “where will the training data come from?” Language models could be trained on huge existing corpuses of text. GPT-3, for instance, was trained on about 300 billion tokens’ worth of data scraped from the internet . In 2024 Epoch AI estimated the total existing stock of quality, accessible text data is around 300 trillion tokens . But there’s no equivalent stock of good, public robot training data. Figuring out where the data for robotics will come from and how much of it we’ll need has become a key problem for the field. 
 What is robot training data? 

 Training an AI model, whether it’s an LLM or a model used for controlling robots, fundamentally requires lots and lots of examples of something being done correctly (or enough knowledge of what a correct action looks like that you can score an attempt). You feed a model some set of inputs, you compare the model’s output to the actual, correct output, and you use the difference between the two to modify the parameters of the model to reduce this difference via some flavor of gradient descent. (We went into this process in some depth earlier this month .) 
 With text, a training example can be just an existing chunk of text; you take the chunk, feed the model part of it, and have the model predict what comes next in the sequence. One chunk of text can thus generate many training examples by feeding the model different portions of the text.

 Via Opus 5.5 
 To train a robot, an example is typically some sort of recording of a task being accomplished or attempted. There are a variety of different methods used to create these recordings and turn them into training data.
 Historically, one of the most common methods for creating these recordings is called “teleoperation.” With teleoperation, a person manipulates some controls that are directly connected to a robot, and uses them to move a robot through some particular task (folding a shirt, picking up and moving objects, and so on). The image below, for instance, shows a person teleoperating a pair of robot arms, using them to pour water from a pitcher into a glass.

 Via Wu et al. 2023 . 
 As the person performs the task, data of the robot’s state is recorded, along with images from cameras that are recording the task (often mounted to the robot itself), which are combined to create a training example. An example might consist of feeding a model some data on the robot’s state and what the cameras can see at some particular point in the sequence. The model will try to predict what the next robot actions are (in the form of something like joint angles), and then compare that to what the actual actions were. This sort of training strategy, using examples generated by an expert (a human) and then training a model to mimic them, is known as behavioral cloning , and it’s part of the broader category of “imitation learning.” 

 Via Opus 5.5. 
 The major benefit of this method is that the data it generates very closely matches the robot’s actual movement capabilities, because it’s produced from the actual robot you want to train, and it remains widely used today. You sometimes see videos or pictures from huge data collection factories , filled with people doing nothing but teleoperating robots to complete various tasks, and you sometimes see teleoperation accidents when the person forgets that they’re controlling the robot and suddenly moves in a way that the robot can’t handle. 
 But teleoperation has drawbacks. The biggest one is probably expense: to get the data, you need to have an actual robot, along with a person to sit there and manipulate it, which makes it hard to scale this method to produce very large amounts of data. Another drawback is that it’s somewhat clunky for a person to control a robot this way; they often can’t get any sort of tactile feedback from the object being manipulated to guide their motions, so this won’t necessarily produce really good data on dexterous manipulation. The clunkiness can also make it hard to collect good teleoperation data, and as I understand it, only a fraction of total teleoperation data collected ends up being useful for training.
 It’s also possible to generate training data using an existing robot by having it move around on its own, recording its actions, and using some sort of system to categorize these behavioral sequences as successful or not. This sort of strategy, where the robot moves autonomously and you score its actions in some way, is known as “reinforcement learning.” Google was doing something like this around 2016 with an “arm farm”: a series of robot arms moving autonomously , with success scored by whether an arm picked up or grasped an object or not. 
 This sort of arm farm doesn’t seem to be widely used today, but using real-world deployed robots will probably eventually be the largest source of robot training data. You often hear robotics folks talk about the need for a “data flywheel,” where you have robots deployed in the real world, you use the data from them to train even better models, resulting in more robot deployments, resulting in better models, and so on.
 Robotic AI company Physical Intelligence, for instance, is experimenting with this sort of “deploy a robot to do real work, and use it to gather data to improve the model” proto-flywheel at a chocolate factory near its offices. But while this might work eventually, right now there’s a very small number of AI-controlled robots doing anything resembling real work. Real-world data also tends to be extremely repetitive — robots doing the same task over and over again in very similar conditions — which makes it much less useful for training. So for now most data needs to come from other sources. 
 Another very common strategy for generating robotic training data is simulation: instead of running a physical robot in the real world, run a digital one in a simulated world instead. This is also commonly done with reinforcement learning, in which you have the robot try to navigate some simulated space, after which you score it on how well it does. Another flavor of this is the teacher/student paradigm where you have a “teacher” model that has full access to all the data within the simulated environment, and a “student” model that can only see what the real robot would see (images from its cameras and its own internal state), and you try to get the student to mimic the teacher. 
 The benefits of simulation are that it’s much cheaper and faster to simulate a robot than it is to deploy one in the real world, and that you can get very large amounts of data by simulating the behavior of thousands of robots. Figure AI taught its humanoid robot to walk via reinforcement learning on thousands of simulated robots . OpenAI taught a robotic hand to solve a Rubik’s Cube via the equivalent of 13,000 years of simulated experience . By 2019, Waymo had driven 10 million miles in the real world, but 10 billion miles in simulation . 

 Via Figure. 
 But simulation also has drawbacks. The biggest one is that the quality of the data it produces is a function of the accuracy of the simulation. For some things, such as locomotion (having a robot move through some physical space), simulation is fairly accurate, because the behavior is mostly well-characterized enough that we can represent it digitally. So you can train a quadruped robot to navigate some terrain, or a bipedal robot to dance, or a drone to fly along some path in simulation, and the simulated environment will be a reasonably good approximation of the real world. But for things such as object manipulation (particularly for soft or deformable objects), it’s much harder to build a simulation that accurately captures real-world behavior, and using it as a source of training data is much more difficult. This is generally referred to as the “ sim-to-real gap .” 
 Because of simulation inaccuracies, one popular simulation technique is called “domain randomization.” Since you don’t know what various parameters like friction will be in the real world, you decide to vary the parameters randomly when running the simulation, in the hopes of creating a training envelope that encompasses the parameters that will be encountered in the real world. When OpenAI trained its robotic hand to solve a Rubik’s Cube, for instance, it continuously expanded the range of parameters being tested, forcing the model to learn to solve the cube in more and more difficult environments, in the hopes of giving it capabilities that would generalize to the real world. The robotics team there used this strategy specifically because accurate physical modeling was too difficult : 
 The biggest challenge we faced was to create environments in simulation diverse enough to capture the physics of the real world. Factors like friction, elasticity and dynamics are incredibly difficult to measure and model for objects as complex as Rubik’s Cubes or robotic hands and we found that domain randomization alone is not enough. 
 To overcome this, we developed a new method called Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR), which endlessly generates progressively more difficult environments in simulation. This frees us from having an accurate model of the real world, and enables the transfer of neural networks learned in simulation to be applied to the real world. 

 But despite this technique, and really quite a lot of simulation (13,000 simulated years), performance was only OK — for maximally difficult starting arrangements, success was only 20% — which is maybe why this technique doesn’t seem to be widely used for manipulation training currently. Environmental parameter randomization does seem to be fairly standard for locomotion training though, used for things like randomly varying the coefficient of friction of the surface being traversed. 
 Another way to use simulation is to create synthetic training data: taking real training data as an input and using it to create lots of similar training examples. Nvidia, for instance, has a page showing how you can use its Isaac GR00T robot simulation platform to generate synthetic robot training data using teleoperation data as an input. Synthetic data generation also seems to be widely used for training autonomous vehicles . 
 We can also create robot training data by recording humans doing tasks and creating some sort of mapping from the recorded task data to a robot. This might be as simple as fitting a person with a head-mounted camera (creating what’s known as “egocentric video”), and using it to record the person doing various tasks with their hands. The video is then processed so the person’s motions are interpreted as robot motions, and used to create training examples. (This is one reason for the popularity of the humanoid form factor in robotics: humans doing tasks will be a reasonably good source of training data.) Figure AI, for instance, recently announced Index, its program for gathering training data by sending people head-mounted cameras to record themselves doing various tasks (alternatively, Figure will also send a crew equipped with these cameras to your home or business to do chores for you). Companies like Shift are also doing this, sending people with hat-mounted cameras to folks’ houses to record themselves doing chores. 

 Figure collecting egocentric video data, via Figure . 
 You can also add more sensors to collect more elaborate human task data. The now-defunct startup Eidon AI stuck sensors on folks’ arms to collect data on joint positions for training robot arms. Neocambrian AI recently announced “Mitts,” which it describes as “state of the art tactile gloves that capture high-precision touch data for robots.” 

 Via X . 

 Via X . 
 Another flavor of this strategy is to use what’s called a Universal Manipulation Interface , or UMI. With UMI, a person holds a pair of specially designed manipulators, that typically have a simple pincer grip at the end (though some have multiple fingers ). The person does various tasks with the manipulators, which have cameras mounted to them that record exactly what the manipulators are doing. This video is then used to create training examples for robots that have similar manipulators. Robot model startups Generalist AI and Sunday Robotics , for instance, seem to be primarily using this strategy for gathering training data. 

 UMI data collection, via Chi et al 2024 . 
 The benefit of using human-collected task data is that it’s much cheaper to collect than teleoperation data: all you need is a camera, or some camera-equipped manipulators, rather than an entire robot. And it’s probably much easier to get a wide variety of different task recordings by just having a person walk around and do different things, rather than constantly repositioning a robot to do them. And because the data is gathered in the real world, by people manipulating real objects, it’s more accurate than simulation. The drawback is that because this data comes from a person rather than a robot, it won’t map onto a robot’s capabilities perfectly well. Even UMI, which minimizes the human-robot differences by having a person do tasks using a sort of robotic manipulator, won’t necessarily map perfectly, though the discrepancy is small and many of the most impressive robotics demos we’ve seen (such as Generalist AI’s ) have been trained using UMI data. 
 It’s possible to use multiple training strategies in a single robot model. Agility Robotics’ Digit , for instance, walks using a small, 1-million-parameter neural network trained in simulation using reinforcement learning, while object manipulation seems to have been trained using behavioral cloning. Dyna Robotics similarly seems to have used a combination of reinforcement learning in simulation and human video for training data. 
 It’s also often useful to train a robot model using data other than task completion data. Vision-Language-Action models, as we noted earlier , are built from Vision-Language models trained on text and images. And because of the ubiquity of general video data on things like YouTube or TikTok, there’s a great deal of interest in finding ways to use generic web videos as a source of training data. Nvidia’s Cosmos , for instance, is a foundation model trained on video data that can be used as a backbone for a robot policy model. 
 Robot dataset sizes 

 How much of this action-relevant training data have we gathered so far, and how much do we need?
 As with AI models more generally, robotic training datasets seem to be getting bigger. An early dataset that was considered large at the time was the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset, created by Google DeepMind in 2023 by stitching together dozens of existing datasets. OXE consisted of over 1 million robot action trajectories spanning more than 160,000 different tasks, totaling around 4,000 hours’ worth of task data. Physical Intelligence’s Pi 0 model , released in October of 2024, was trained on 10,000 hours of robot task data. Generalist AI’s GEN 1 model, announced earlier this year, was trained on around 500,000 hours of UMI data. Figure AI’s Index announcement stated that it was gathering 30 minutes of video data every second, or 43,000 hours a day. Dyna Robotics and Nature Will both claim that their latest models are trained on over 1 million hours of human egocentric video data. I saw one estimate that the robot data collection industry is collectively producing around 500,000 hours of training data a month. 
 It’s not amazingly easy to compare these datasets to the text datasets used to train LLMs. For one, different robotic training sets have different levels of quality — teleoperation or UMI data is probably higher quality than pure human egocentric video data for training purposes, and a highly variable dataset with lots of different sorts of tasks is much more useful than an extremely repetitive one. And video data for robotics training is informationally different from text data: for one, video data tends to be highly correlated — on short timescales, one frame is pretty similar to another frame. Thus even though video recordings contain a lot of data, they’re probably less informationally dense for training purposes than text data. As robotics researcher Chris Paxton notes , “ machine learning works best the closer you are to an independent and identically distributed (iid) dataset; neither web data nor robot data is iid, but robot data is far less iid than web data. ” 
 But we can still try. One very rough way to compare dataset sizes, used by Physical Intelligence researcher Kevin Black in this presentation and robotics professor Ken Goldberg in this presentation , is to estimate how long a dataset of text tokens would take for a person to read, and compare that to the hours of robot training data we have. Under this metric, GPT-2 was trained on the equivalent of around 475,000 hours of data, GPT-3 was trained on 26 million hours, and GPT-4 was trained on 685 million hours. Using this (somewhat dubious) metric, even several-year-old AI models were trained on something like 1,000 times the data that current robot models are trained on. Goldberg calls this the “ 100,000-year data gap .” 

 On his Substack “It Can Think!” Paxton makes an estimate from the other direction , converting hours of robot data into tokens, and comes up with a fairly similar estimate. He guesses that for video running at 10 frames per second, each frame will contain the equivalent of one useful training token. Every hour of video thus contains about 10 × 60 × 60 = 36,000 training tokens. He then divides this by 10, on the assumption that you can’t expect every second of video data to actually be useful, getting you about 3,600 training tokens per hour of video. This would make Dyna Robotics’s 1 million hours of training equal to around 3.6 billion tokens, on the order of 5,000 times smaller than the datasets used to train recent AI models. 
 If you assume that we need about 1,000 times as much real-world robot training data as we actually have — on the order of 1 billion hours of data — how much would that cost to generate? Eidon AI reports robot data prices of $60 to $100 per hour. Pantheon similarly reports UMI data costs from $60 to $150 per hour, but that its engineers developed a pipeline capable of generating data for $10 per hour. Shift claims you can earn up to $20 per hour recording egocentric video data and selling it to them. So a naive estimate suggests that getting enough data to get robot models to a similar level of capability as recent AI models might cost on the order of $10 billion to $100 billion. 
 How much data we actually need, however, seems to be something of an open question. If we don’t care about generalization, and only want to train robot models to perform various specific tasks, the data requirements might be substantially less. Alternatively, it might turn out that frontier general AI models like Fable or Astra are extremely capable robot controllers after being fed a comparatively small amount of robotic training data; some folks are already predicting that special-purpose robot AI models might get replaced with more generic AI models. Likewise, we might find robotic AI model architectures that are much more sample efficient — learning much more from each training example — than current models.
 On the other hand, it could be the case that moving around and manipulating objects in the real world is a fundamentally more difficult task than manipulating text on a computer, and that models require a lot more training to become competent at this real-world behavior. Small errors in generated text, for instance — misspellings, or slightly wrong word choice — don’t necessarily compromise the text as a whole, but when you’re manipulating objects a single tiny positioning error can be the difference between success and complete failure. Likewise, Ken Goldberg argues that robotic movement is a much higher-dimensional problem than generating text (though I’m not sure I buy the version of the argument he articulates in that presentation, but I have seen this sentiment echoed by other robotics researchers). 
 Conclusion 

 LLMs showed the benefits of scale when training AI models — bigger models, more data, trained using much more computation. We’re now in the process of seeing how well this scaling works for robotics, and robotics researchers are working hard to gather enormous amounts of robot task training data in the hopes of seeing the same sort of trends with robotic capabilities that we have with AI more generally.
```

### C. Expedia amazoned hotel rooms（Cory Doctorow / Pluralistic）

```text
Expedia amazoned hotel rooms ( permalink )

 No matter where you shop, you're getting screwed by Amazon. Amazon has found a way to make everything you buy – at Target, at a mom-and-pop shop, even direct from the manufacturer – more expensive. And they're not the only one: Expedia's done the same thing, but for hotel rooms.

 Here's how Amazon broke the economy: first, they locked in a key group of shoppers. The vast majority of affluent shoppers in the USA have Amazon Prime accounts. Having prepaid for a year's shipping in advance, those Prime subscribers start their purchasing journey by searching Amazon, and if they find what they're looking for, that's where it ends.

 America's affluent top decile – the 10% of richest Americans – do the majority of America's shopping, a phenomenon that has only been accelerated by America's precipitous slide into oligarchy, which has exhausted the savings and the credit-card limits and the home equity of the majority of Americans, who increasingly can't afford to buy anything:

 https://dreamagainusa.org/ 

 If you want to sell things in America, you must sell them in the place where the top decile shops, and that's Amazon. For merchants, that is an incredibly expensive proposition: 51-60% of every dollar you spend with an independent merchant on Amazon is claimed by Amazon through junk fees, listing fees, commissions, processing fees and other scams:

 https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/04/cheating-at-fraud/#absentee-rentier 

 Merchants don't have 51-60% margins they can afford to turn over to Amazon. To remain viable while selling on Amazon, they have to raise prices. Amazon doesn't mind if prices go up, so long as Amazon's price remains competitive. So Amazon imposes a requirement on its suppliers called "most favored nation," which requires sellers to offer their best price on Amazon:

 https://pluralistic.net/2026/02/25/most-favored-nation/#price-fixing 

 Practically speaking, that means that when you raise your price on Amazon, you must raise your prices everywhere by the same amount, which means that no matter where you shop (Target, Walmart, the corner store or direct from the factory), you pay the Amazon Tax.

 The Amazon Tax is a prime (you should forgive the expression) example of enshittification, in which an intermediary (a business that sits between buyers and sellers, AKA, "a platform") screws both customers and sellers to benefit itself.

 But Amazon isn't the only example of a powerful digital intermediary that has used its market power to impose an economy-wide tax. Wherever you look online, you find digital giants who grew the way Amazon grew (buying all of their competitors) and then did what Amazon did (squeezing buyers and sellers, making prices higher everywhere).

 That's what happened with Expedia. Almost every travel site you can find online has been purchased by Expedia (Hotels.com, Travelocity, and Orbitz), and the ones that aren't Expedia subsidiaries were acquired by its main rival, Priceline (Booking.com, Kayak, Agoda and even Opentable). Nearly all the hotel rooms are booked by a tiny minority of travelers – largely that same top decile that Amazon has so thoroughly captured and locked in – and hoteliers can't live without their business, and they're almost all Expedia and/or Priceline customers. Expedia accounts for 80% of all hotel bookings by dollar value.

 Expedia's position, in other words, is basically Amazon's position, so Expedia is doing exactly what Amazon is doing: they charge huge fees to hotels, and then force the hotels to raise their prices everywhere through a "most favored nation" deal:

 https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.wawd.369601/gov.uscourts.wawd.369601.1.0.pdf 

 But – as Lee Hepner writes for the BIG newsletter – Expedia has figured out ways to out-Amazon Amazon. They've copied one of Amazon's sleaziest businesses, and made it worse :

 https://www.thebignewsletter.com/p/how-expedia-rigs-travel-markets-and 

 Here's Amazon's sleaziest commercial practice: when you search Amazon, the top results for your search aren't the best matches for your queries; they're products sold by the companies willing to pay the most for search placement, in a "real-time auction" that runs every time you search. The cost of winning that auction comes out of your pocket through higher prices: the first result for an Amazon query is, on average, 29% more expensive than the best match. The top row is 25% more expensive. On average, the best match for your query is 17 positions down the screen, on the second page, where almost no one ever bothers to scroll:

 https://pluralistic.net/2023/11/03/subprime-attention-rent-crisis/#euthanize-rentiers 

 This rip-off is Amazon's most profitable line of business, more profitable than AWS or Prime. It makes the company more than $80b/year . And – incredibly – Expedia's found a way to make it even worse.

 Like Amazon, Expedia auctions off its search results. They call this their "VIP listing" program. Practically speaking, hotels have to pay for this, because it boosts booking rates by 42% (the first ten results on an Expedia search-results page get 62% of the clicks). VIP listing is expensive for the merchant – but it's even more expensive for you and me.

 That's because Expedia requires hotels in its VIP listings programs to raise their prices on non-Expedia sites by 10-25%…INDEFINITELY. 

 Let me repeat that: to stay in business, hotels need to be on Expedia. To stay in business on Expedia, hotels have to raise their prices to pay Expedia's junk fees, but they have to raise their prices everywhere else. But to really stay in business on Expedia, hotels have to use the VIP program, which is even more expensive. And – one more time – hotels in the VIP program have to jack up their prices everywhere else, INDEFINITELY . This means that, throughout the economy, hotel prices are rising at a superinflationary rate, because Expedia – who command 80% of the market – are pushing those prices up.

 This goes a long way to explaining why US hotel rates rose 58% from a 2020 average of $103/night to a 2025 average of $162/night. It explains why hotel prices rose by 5% last year , which is double the rate of inflation. And this is why Expedia stock is up 65% since the start of the Iran war, a fact that Expedia CEO Ariane Gorin attributes to "higher margins" from "higher prices":

 https://s202.q4cdn.com/757635260/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/Q2-2026-Earnings-Presentation-Final.pdf 

 In his coverage of the Expedia lawsuit – which brought many of these details to light – Hepner notes that hotels themselves have undergone near-total cartelization, with Hilton, Marriott, and Choice International owning the majority of all hotels after a string of acquisitions that went largely unchallenged by slumbering antitrust regulators around the world. These consolidated chains have also jacked up prices and enshittified their service, slashing housekeeping and check-in staff and allowing their facilities to slip into disrepair.

 This, too, is characteristic of monopolization: whenever a monopoly takes over part of its supply chain, the rest of the supply chain consolidates to prevent itself from being ripped off by the new giant. This is the story of publishers consolidating to defend themselves from Borders/B&N, Walmart and Costco, then Amazon; and it's the story of literary agencies consolidating to resist the power of those overgrown publishers.

 And it's the story of health-care, which saw consolidation in pharmaceuticals leading to mergers among hospitals, who screwed the insurers, who also consolidated.

 In every case, it's the workers and the people who rely on them that get the shitty end of the stick. Hotel guests, readers and patients pay more for inferior products. Writers, editors, doctors, nurses, hotel staff and housekeepers get lower pay and worse working-conditions:

 https://pluralistic.net/2024/12/18/loose-flapping-ends/#luigi-has-a-point 

 There is nothing wrong with the idea of an intermediary. Most writers can't publish and sell their own books, and few travelers can navigate finding hotels in strange cities without help. The problem of intermediaries is that when intermediaries gain monopoly power – by buying their rivals and capturing buyers and sellers – they are able to squeeze both. As Tim Wu says, this is the moment when a company that's supposed to be helping becomes a predator, acquires "main character syndrome" and makes every transaction all about the middleman, at the expense of the people buying and selling:

 https://pluralistic.net/2026/08/05/absentee-landlord/ 

 There are so many reasons for inflation and the cost of living crisis: wage stagnation, wars of choice in Iran, environmental cataclysm after environmental cataclysm. But under cover of these real factors is a cohort of what Matt Stoller calls "economic termites," parasites that have infested the walls of our economy and are devouring it from within.

 You can't solve this by shopping. Calling the hotel direct won't fix it: they have to be on Expedia to survive, and to be on Expedia, they have to sign up for VIP service, and to keep VIP service, they have to make their prices higher everywhere. To fix this, we need systemic action: the corporate death penalty for the companies that are consuming the structural members of our society.
```

### D. Token Ring network（Dave Farquhar / Silicon Underground）

```text
Token Ring is an obsolete networking technology that competed with Ethernet in the 1980s and 1990s. IBM first announced it October 15, 1985. It is more of a curiosity today, as the networking standard that lost. But when Token Ring equipment turns up, it can look enough like Ethernet to confuse people. And why it lost can be interesting to study.
 Ethernet versus Token Ring
 This Intel Token Ring card can pass for Ethernet. The best indicator from this photo is the DE-9 connector on the back. Ethernet never used that. Even though it was an IBM technology, companies like Intel and 3Com wanted in on the game. Token Ring gets a bad rap today, and that isn’t necessarily fair. Token Ring was a standard backed by IBM. Ethernet was an open standard backed by DEC , 3Com and others. Of course Ethernet won.
 The main reason Ethernet won the war was because it was cheaper. Once you bought everything you needed, Ethernet cost a third as much. Whether Ethernet was half as good or 75% as good was always a matter of debate, but for a third the price, it only needed to be half as good to be a reasonable value.
 Token Ring had one big weakness besides cost. It ran at specific speeds, and unlike Ethernet, all the cards on the ring had to talk at the same speed. If you had a 16 megabit network and brought up a system at 4 megabits, it took down the whole network.
 I know this from experience. But I only made that mistake once.
 But as long as you didn’t do that, Token Ring was extremely robust. it didn’t have a problem with collisions, unlike early Ethernet. The way Token Ring worked was the systems passed a token around the network. If you didn’t have the token, you didn’t get to talk. That meant no collisions. And it handled saturation much better.
 I brought down an Ethernet network once just by trying to clone a classroom full of systems using Symantec Ghost’s multicast feature. I did it all the time when I worked in a Token Ring shop. The first time I did that on an Ethernet network, I took down all of the networking in the building. They had to isolate the classroom behind a bridge to allow me to do that. That was 24 years ago and I guarantee there are two people who who still hold that against me even though none of us work there anymore.
 Why Ethernet won
 Ethernet was junk if you went with the cheapest options all around, but you could make it acceptable if you invested some of the savings in improved technology. Replacing hubs with switches mostly eliminated the collision problem. Buying higher quality cards cut down on the reliability problem.
 But the other reason Ethernet won was because of scale. It scaled to higher speeds first, so it won the war through brute force. Ethernet made it to 100 megabits first. Anyone who saw 16 megabit Token Ring in action can tell you it was way more than 60% faster than 10 megabit Ethernet, but 16 megabit Token Ring couldn’t keep up with 100 megabit Ethernet. And when 100 megabit Token Ring came out, the next iteration was going to be 128 megabit. Gigabit Ethernet came out before 128 megabit Token Ring ever saw the light of day.
 And at gigabit speeds, the advantage of Token Ring over Ethernet was mostly academic. Maybe the network itself was a bit more robust in some ways, but the better efficiency didn’t matter much. The bus the cards plugged into and the disks in the system were bigger bottlenecks.
 In 2022, we have a faster bus and SSDs so we’d be able to tell a difference, but then we’d be back at that problem of scale.
 Telling the difference between a Token Ring and Ethernet card today
 Whenever a retro computing Youtuber does a mail call that includes batches of random ISA cards, inevitably, when faced with a mystery network card, they will wonder aloud into the camera whether it is Ethernet or Token Ring. Telling the difference can be difficult. Even in the ’90s when both technologies were viable, professionals sometimes made the mistake. Plugging Ethernet into a Token Ring network because they were both CAT5 is just as fun as bringing up a node at 4 megabits on a 16 megabit network. And by fun, I mean destructive.
 Yes, by the mid-90s, both technologies could run on RJ45 cabling. The pinout was different, but you could use the same cable for either. If you want to try to tell the difference by toning it out with a multimeter, what you need to know is that on Token Ring, the outer pins weren’t connected. On Ethernet from the same time period, the two center pins were the unconnected pins.
 The D-sub connectors
 But it’s usually easier to distinguish the two by looking at the other connectors. Ethernet used a 15-pin connector that looks quite a bit like a PC joystick connector. Token Ring used a 9-pin connector that looks like a 9-pin video connector or an Atari style joystick plug.
 Coax connections
 Coax connectors for Token Ring did exist, but were more common on Ethernet networks. The presence of a coax connector on a card isn’t a reliable indicator of either network type. But it was more common with Ethernet than Token Ring, so if I see a coax connector on an unknown card, it tilts it in favor of Ethernet.
 Speed indicators
 The LEDs and other printing on the card can be a very good indicator. The majority of ISA Ethernet cards you will find ran at 10 megabits. PCI cards ran at 10 and 100 megabits. So look for any speed indicator legends that might be printed or embossed on the card, either on the backplate or on the PCB itself.
 Token Ring ran at 4 megabits and 16 megabits. So on an ISA card, if you see both a link activity light and a speed light, and the light indicates a speed of 4 or 16, that’s Token Ring. If there is a dip switch to set a speed of 4 or 16 megabits, that’s Token Ring.
 Brand
 You can’t necessarily go by brand. IBM sold Ethernet cards, and Intel and 3Com did make Token Ring cards. So don’t automatically assume a 3Com or Intel ISA card that looks like a network card is Ethernet.
 There were other obscure makers of Token Ring cards, including Thomas Conrad and Madge.
 Is Token Ring hardware worth messing with today?
 I liked Token Ring when it was still on the market, though I might have thought differently if I had been in on the yearly computing budget. But once it was becoming clear that Token Ring was the Betamax of networking , people started replacing it with Ethernet. Since the cards took up less space, they were less likely to get scrapped than the equipment in the racks or wiring closets.
 It’s easier to find the cards today than the necessary supporting infrastructure, like a MAU, the equivalent of an Ethernet switch. And bridging Ethernet and Token Ring is more difficult than just slapping one of each card in a Linux box and configuring the two cards to route.
 That’s not to say using it is impossible today. It’s impractical and difficult. But if that’s your idea of fun, don’t let me dissuade you. It’s not my idea of fun. But I don’t think coax-based Ethernet is worth messing with either, and there are people who do that.
 Why anyone messed with Token Ring at all
 One question I hear sometimes is why anyone messed with Token Ring at all, since Ethernet was an open standard, and open standards are better.
 It helps to remember that the IBM of the 1980s was not the same company that IBM is today . Not only you were they a dominant player in the personal computer market, they were also the dominant player in the minicomputer and mainframe markets, which, at the time, was what some people considered serious computing. It was the preferred networking option for IBM minicomputers and mainframes.
 There was a saying in the 1980s that nobody got fired for choosing IBM. That alone gave Token Ring a chance, for a few years.
 ARCNET was another competitor from the same timeframe. It, too, was a contender for a while because it was backed by Datapoint , a pioneer in office automation using small computers.
 Are Ethernet and Token Ring interchangeable or interoperable?
 Sometimes you will see cards and hubs listed on eBay as both, which may lead someone to believe the two are interchangeable, or that some kind of dual-standard cards and hubs existed for a time. Don’t believe the listing. Network cards were one or the other, not both. Hubs and switches are Ethernet. The equivalent equipment for Token Ring was called a MAU.
 Unless you can tell from looking at the photos what it is, don’t buy it. And if they listed it as both, assume it’s untested. If the price is good and the photographs permit identification, you may be able to snag a bargain.
```

```text
 The “C” word
 lcamtuf 
 ·

 Read full story 

 My goal wasn’t to say “you’re wrong”; it was to show that philosophy is interesting and that the conclusions reached by some well-known tech thinkers are not as complete or inescapable as they seem.
 Long-time readers might remember that these aren’t this blog’s first forays into philosophy. For example, in 2025, I covered the origins and consequences of mathematical abstractions ; a bit later, I explored the connection between Gödel and the theory of computability . Don’t get me wrong: the field has a propensity for navel-gazing and petty semantic squabbles. That said, philosophy has given us the foundations of mathematics and most of modern science — and while it occasionally peddles mysticism and promises false certainty, it’s still the purest way to examine meaning, existence, reason, and truth. 
 …for some definition of truth 

 Truth appears to be the fundamental goal for philosophical inquiry. It’s not hard to see why: falsehoods are dime a dozen but lack epistemological value. In contrast, the uppercase Truth — taken as a whole — is the singularly correct account of reality.
 Of course, that’s just a slogan; similarly to mathematicians, modern philosophers tend to be more analytical. A mathematician is not content with the definition of a number as “something we can count to” ; they want to pinpoint the essential characteristics of numbers and then apply this knowledge to new realms. In the same vein, most philosophers wish to define uppercase Truth as something more than just a synonym for reality. 
 Whatever the nature of this singular Truth might be, we seem to experience it through individual value judgments; we hold that some things are true and others are false. These plentiful lowercase truths are not standalone beings; instead, they appear to be bivalent (two-valued) attributes that attach to statements made in a language of our choice. An apple, in the sense of a physical object, can’t be “true” or “false”; a statement about apples might. 
 Of course, not every utterance can be a truth-bearer (a proposition ). For one, the candidate phrase must be unambiguously interpretable. It must also make a factual declaration of some sort; “I am holding an apple” is fine — it gives us a binary claim we can examine — but “please hand me an apple” won’t do. 
 In an earlier article on paradoxes , I also remarked that self-referential statements, such as “this sentence is false” , might need to be taken out of the equation. In the same write-up, I’ve given an even wackier example of Curry’s paradox. It’s a sentence that doesn’t seem to contradict itself, but forces us to accept an arbitrarily-chosen consequent (the B in “ if A then B” ) as true: 
 “Imagine the following seemingly innocent sentence: 
 \(p = \text{“if }p\text{ is true, then pigs can fly”}\) 

 Considered as a whole, an “if A then B” statement can’t possibly be true if the antecedent (A) is true while the consequent (B) is not. For example, if I say “if it’s Wednesday, then I’m at my mom’s”, and it’s Wednesday and you catch me partying, my earlier claim couldn’t have been correct. 
 Circling back to Curry’s sentence, this tells us that p, taken as a whole, can’t possibly be assigned the value of truth unless pigs are actually able to fly: if p equals true, this automatically renders the antecedent true and requires the consequent to hold. Without forming an opinion about the logic value of p, we can formalize this conclusion in the form of a new statement, q: 
 \(q = \text{“if }p\text{ is true, then pigs can fly”}\) 

 But hold on: q is a necessary truth, but it also says precisely the same thing as p. Therefore, p is also necessarily true; pigs must be able to fly.” 

 Paradoxes of self-reference can be kept at bay by placing constraints on the object language — that is, the language we use to build propositions. This language can be more restricted than the meta-language we use to discuss their meaning.
 At this point, we have a fairly agreeable definition of truth as a semantic attribute; to build on this, in another article , I outlined the usual formal rules of manipulating propositions to derive new facts. For example, it makes sense to codify that when we write “p = a AND b” , this means that p is true if and only if both a and b are true. The resulting truth-algebra can be tinkered with to taste, but right now, we have a far more pressing issue to solve. 
 …for some definitions of reality 

 So far, we’ve defined truth as a semantic attribute of propositions and then endowed it all with an algebra. This makes mathematicians happy but lacks any notion of truthmaking : there’s no process, other than whimsy, by which we decide that “ 2 + 2 = 4” ought to be true and “pigs can fly” ought to be not. 
 An instinctive approach is to adopt the correspondence theory : that is, the belief that semantic truth must correspond to facts . This, however, creates as many problems as it solves. We now need to decide the meaning of facts: are they just ideas we can express with words — a collection of sentences and attached judgments? If yes, what distinguishes facts from any other set of semantic truths taken as axioms? Do we need this at all if facts and truths look pretty much the same?… 
 Conversely, if facts are separate from semantic truths… what are they, exactly? A more nebulous, non-linguistic property of reality? Can you see a fact? When you’re looking at a cat on a sofa, how do you know it’s a cat and a sofa, and not some singular, Cronenberg-type cat-sofa entity? Do we need a theory of cathood first? 

 The questions may seem capricious, but under the correspondence theory, it’s hard to say anything useful about facts without first building a seemingly unattainable meta-model of mind and reality. Without it, facts just are . 
 Because of this issue, some thinkers reject the correspondence theory and hold that uppercase Truth is more or less inscrutable: it’s the end of the line for philosophical inquiry. In the most radical ( deflationist ) view, any mention of truth is just syntactic sugar; “<x> is true” is the same as saying “suppose that <x>” or simply “<x>” . In this view, the only requirement for philosophical investigations is that they need to start with internally coherent axioms; that’s to say, in conventional logic, we can’t simultaneously assert that “pigs can fly” and “pigs cannot fly” . Beyond this, we can construct any universe we like; truth is just an outcome of a thought process that follows that universe’s rules. 
 This is essentially the approach of mathematical logic; we choose axioms based on intuition and never look back. The method works surprisingly well. For example, it’s given us a good theory of infinite quantities, with applications to calculus.
 …for some definitions of inquiry 

 The discussion in the preceding section might have biased you toward the view that all this talk of a singular, uppercase Truth is just a waste of time. If so, there’s a simple thought experiment that might change your mind.
 Say that I own a 12-pound cat named Cindy Clawford; you wish to inquire about her weight. I can answer in a number of ways. In particular, I can say that Cindy weighs “less than, equal to, or more than 1,000 pounds” . This is a truth, but it’s a logical tautology that holds for any physical object. It’s given you no information whatsoever. 
 In light of this, it would seem foolish to reduce philosophy solely to the pursuit of semantic, lowercase truths; we’re clearly seeking something more. Now, suppose that our uppercase Truth exists and has the shape of a set T containing all possible true statements about the world. To illustrate, in the Cindy-verse, a slice of the set may look the following way:
 \(\begin{align}
\ldots \\
t_{1010} &= \text{“Cindy doesn't weigh 10 pounds.”} \\
t_{1011} &= \text{“Cindy doesn't weigh 11 pounds.”} \\
t_{1012} &= \text{“Cindy weighs 12 pounds.”} \\
t_{1013} &= \text{“Cindy doesn't weigh 13 pounds.”} \\
t_{1014} &= \text{“Cindy doesn't weigh 14 pounds.”} \\
t_{1015} &= \text{“Cindy doesn't weigh 15 pounds.”} \\
\ldots \\
t_{2000} &= \text{“Cindy doesn't weigh 1,000 pounds.”} \\
\ldots

\end{align}\) 

 We don’t concern ourselves with where the contents of T come from. Maybe it’s the scientific theory of everything, maybe we found it in a holy book. The only point is that T — a collection of all truths — is a complete model of reality, at least to the extent that it can be expressed with words.
 Channeling Karl Popper, this allows us to rank semantic truths — which can be viewed basically as competing descriptions (theories) of reality — simply by measuring how well they cover T. That is to say, we can assess the theories’ uppercase Truthlikeness . Consider the following: 
 \(\begin{align}
c_{1} &= \text{“Cindy weighs less than, equal to, or more than 1,000 pounds.”} \\
c_{2} &= \text{“Cindy weighs less than 1,000 pounds.”} \\
c_{3} &= \text{“Cindy weighs less than 14 pounds.”} \\
c_{4} &= \text{“Cindy weighs 12 pounds.”}

\end{align}\) 

 In this example, the tautological theory c 1 is true, but doesn’t entail (confirm) any statements listed in our earlier subset of T-truths. So, we have a practical indication of the theory’s worthlessness. 
 Statement c 2 — “Cindy weighs less than 1,000 pounds” — gets us farther: it lets us infer the truth of t 2000 , so it’s a measurably better theory of T. This continues with statement c 3 — “Cindy weighs less than 14 pounds” — which additionally establishes the truth of t 1014 and t 1015 . Finally, statement c 4 is a complete theory of what’s shown in the earlier snippet of T. 
 Again, this approach allows us to rate empirical statements based on how well they approximate the whole Truth of T. This sounds like a better way to frame rational inquiry: we’re not merely collecting truth-Pokémon, we’re trying to get close to some sort of philosophical divinity. Of course, we’re leaving many other questions unanswered; for example, we don’t have a metric of usefulness or similarity of the statements entitled by different theories, so it’s not necessarily a given that a theory with higher T-truth content is worth our time.
 Another way to illustrate the shortcomings of this model is to consider the problem of invalid theories. The earlier statement that Cindy weighs less than 1,000 pounds is a valid theory of T, but it is ranks low in the T-truth hierarchy: it describes reality rather imprecisely. In contrast, the claim that Cindy weighs eleven pounds is invalid with respect to T — it contradicts reality — but it’s very close. In particular, in the earlier snippet, it selects for many of the same T-truths as our most accurate valid theory, c 4 . So, can we build a partial Truthlikeness ordering of invalid theories, too? 
 Alas, a simple ranking appears to be unattainable. To explain, let’s assume we have two invalid theories of T — A and B — and that both entail some factually-accurate statements that belong to T; that’s their T-true content. At the same time, because the theories are invalid, they must also entail some statements that contradict T; this is the T-false content of A and B.
 Next, assume that the T-truth content of A is greater than B; in essence, let’s declare that this is the theory that ought to sit higher than B in the hierarchy of Truthlikeness. If so, there must be at least one T-true proposition, a t , which is entailed by A but not B. Further, because A is an invalid theory, it must also entail at least one counter-to-T statement, a f . 
 With this in mind, let’s consider a new statement in the form p = a t ∧ a f . Here, “∧” is the logical conjunction operator (AND), so p is true if and only if both a t and a f are true. This is necessarily the case under A, so we must conclude that p is a logical consequence by this theory. This new statement is T-false because it hinges on the truth of a counter-to-T proposition a f ; in other words, it belongs on the theory’s T-false pile. 
 In contrast, theory B doesn’t entail p because the truth of p hinges on a t , and by our earlier choice, this proposition is not a consequence of B. Therefore, p is a T-falsehood that’s produced only by A. It would seem that it’s not possible for theory A to have larger coverage of uppercase Truth without also incurring more T-falsehoods, which seems to get in the way of ranking one as better than the other. 
 But then, again — is p an interesting consequence of the theory? Or for that matter, are a t and a f ? That question is a wholly separate can of worms.
```

```text
 I noted last time that you are not allowed to ask std:: async for an immediate on-thread calculation. The C++ library designers probably chose not to support it because there’s no point: An immediate on-thread calculation is just a function call, so call the function already and leave me out of it.

 But maybe you want to do it because you are interfacing with a component that wants a std:: future . How could you do it?

 One way would be to implement the immediately synchronous case manually by setting a value into a promise.

 template<typename F, typename... Args>
auto
 make_immediate_future(F&& f, Args&&... args)
-> std::future<
 std::invoke_result_t<std::decay_t<F>,
 std::decay_t<Args>...>>
{
 std::promise<
 std::invoke_result_t<std::decay_t<F>,
 std::decay_t<Args>...>> p;
 try {
 p.set_value(std::invoke(auto(f), auto(args)...));
 } catch (...) {
 p.set_exception(std::current_exception());
 }
 return p.get_future();
}

 Or you could make packaged_task do the work for you:

 template<typename F, typename... Args>
auto
 make_immediate_future(F&& f, Args&&... args)
-> std::future<
 std::invoke_result_t<std::decay_t<F>,
 std::decay_t<Args>...>>
{
 using R = std::invoke_result_t<std::decay_t<F>,
 std::decay_t<Args>...>;

 std::packaged_task<R(Args...)> task(f);
 task(args...);
 return task.get_future();
}

 Yet another way would be to ask for an on-demand in-thread execution, and then immediately demand it.

 template<typename F, typename... Args>
auto
 make_immediate_future(F&& f, Args&&... args)
-> std::future<
 std::invoke_result_t<std::decay_t<F>,
 std::decay_t<Args>...>>
{
 auto f = std::async(std::launch::deferred,
 std::forward<F>(f),
 std::forward<Args>(args)...);
 f.wait();
 return f;
}
```

### H. We Need All the Disciplines（Jim Nielsen）

```text
From where I sit, figuring out what to build, building it, and shipping it are all part of the art of making software.

 Many disciplines have emerged through the years to facilitate this work: product, design, engineering, devops, etc.

 We need all these disciplines to make great, dependable software (granted, in different doses). Each brings different values and judgments; each sees things the others don’t.

 And including all might mean going a bit slower.

 Human collaboration may seem less “efficient” than handing the work over entirely to the machines.

 But inefficiency may be the cost of collaborating with humans, rather than extracting an output from a machine. We gain not only from their judgment and expertise in a particular discipline, but from the relationship, trust, and shared understanding that blossoms from making something together.

 As the old proverb says: “If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together.”

 Reformulated for today: If you want to go fast, go with the LLM. If you want to go far, go with your fellow humans.
```
